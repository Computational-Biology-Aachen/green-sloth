[Farquhar & Busch (2017)](https://doi.org/10.1111/nph.14512) explain the Kok effect, the change in slope of the CO₂ assimilation rate against irradiance near the light compensation point that is usually read as an inhibition of mitochondrial respiration in the light, without any such inhibition. They use the electron-transport-limited rate of the FvCB model with a linear light response of electron transport (Eqns 13, 14) and a total conductance from the air to the chloroplast stroma that rises with irradiance (Eqn 19). In the dark and at low light the conductance is small, so the chloroplastic CO₂ partial pressure $C_c$ is high; as irradiance increases, $C_c$ falls towards half the ambient value. The ratio $\Gamma^*/C_c$, and with it photorespiration, rises with irradiance, which bends the $A$–$I$ response although day respiration $R_d$ is constant. Combining Eqn 14 with the conductance (Eqn 15) gives a quadratic in $A$ (Eqns A5–A9). The same model reproduces the apparent inhibition found with the Laisk method, in which $A$–$C_i$ lines at several irradiances intersect below $-R_d$.

GreenSloth implements the paper's detailed version: $A$ and $C_c$ from the ambient CO₂ and the total conductance, and $A$ at a constant intercellular CO₂ through the mesophyll conductance (the Laisk approach), with the temperature responses of $R_d$ (Heskel et al. 2016) and $\Gamma^*$ (Walker et al. 2013) that the paper uses. It also gives the closed forms of the simplest version (Eqns 16, 17, A1–A3). The defaults are the values of Table 1 at 25 °C ($C_a/P$ = 400 µmol mol⁻¹, $O/P$ = 210 mmol mol⁻¹, $f$ = 0.3, $R_d$ = 1 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹). CO₂ is given as mole fractions ($C/P$). The paper's illustrative data are from sunflower (_Helianthus annuus_); the model itself is generic C₃.
